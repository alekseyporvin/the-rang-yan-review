**Tale of Pearl-Grain Rice**

<div class="poem-verse">
<p>We lived practically on air,<br />
made ourselves small<br />
as a wicker basket<br />
and occupied no more</p>
<p>than a corner, not even a closet<br />
or dresser drawer for our clothes<br />
is how we got through college,<br />
taking out the barest of minimum loans.</p>
<p>It wasn’t that long ago—<br />
earth’s warming became a worry,<br />
over-population a crisis.<br />
We cheered oil embargoes,<br />
convinced solar power would take over—</p>
<p>Not that long ago like when<br />
our grandfathers walked to school<br />
ten miles in snow<br />
in holey shoes. And not so long ago<br />
when our fathers grew up</p>
<p>in three-room shacks<br />
and tore catalog pages in an outhouse—<br />
We predicted shortages, starvation,<br />
extinction, not to mention</p>
<p>wars and bad weather.<br />
We grieved the disappearance<br />
of species and wetlands, mourned<br />
the division of bottom-land,<br />
the clear-cutting of forests.</p>
<p>This is where 20-pound sacks of rice come in,<br />
like organic manna from heaven.<br />
A food subsidy from we-didn’t-care-who.<br />
Save the earth with brown rice!<br />
(Complemented with beans and American cheese.)</p>
<p>Brown rice with its hull dust<br />
that could ignite silos.<br />
And tiny pebbles you better<br />
pick out before cooking<br />
or you’d be adding a dentist</p>
<p>to your list of bad debt.<br />
Rice, day in and day out<br />
(and don’t forget<br />
beans for complete protein).</p>
<p>Cheap college, cheap rent. Plentiful<br />
run-down flats and falling-down houses<br />
to be shared. I could hitchhike<br />
and ride my bike anywhere. Living on rice.</p>
<p>In the end, rice wasn’t so perfect.<br />
It transformed native marsh into miles upon miles<br />
of rice paddies. Pillars of black smoke<br />
burned our eyes every fall.</p>
<p>And then those paddies gave way<br />
to zero lot line tracts, rows of obese homes.<br />
Hello, children. After all that rice<br />
your parents decided they couldn’t be</p>
<p>small anymore. You are tiny<br />
and we are big.<br />
Our houses are big. Our cars are big.<br />
Everything we do is bigger than big.</p>
<p>And if we strike a match all at once,<br />
there will be a very big bang.</p>
</div>

**Rescuing a Brother-in-Law**

<div class="poem-verse">
<p>This is temporary, everyone insists,<br />
Jerry can move into the spare bedroom,<br />
our son’s boxes stacked near a closet full of laundry.<br />
He’s thankful we’re here for him<br />
until his Econoline van is fixed, the prized possession</p>
<p>that holds all his possessions.<br />
The van that serves as home.<br />
My husband opens the hood,<br />
checks wiring and gaskets,<br />
inspects the propane stove</p>
<p>which has the potential to blow up the garage.<br />
Without consulting anyone,<br />
in a fit of do-good zeal mixed with guilt<br />
he decides to gut the van.<br />
Gut Jerry’s home.</p>
<p>My brother-in-law sleeps<br />
in a nylon bag laid atop the bed<br />
refusing to slip between the clean sheets,<br />
tries to pee nightly into a plastic apple juice bottle<br />
though the bathroom is a few steps away.</p>
<p>He takes showers in the backyard,<br />
sorry he broke the faucet in our tub.<br />
This is temporary.<br />
Until his van<br />
is like new.</p>
</div>

**Picture This**

<div class="poem-verse">
<p>The mother with glassy eyes<br />
nursing her baby in Darfur.<br />
She seems to be dreaming<br />
but she’s dreaming of nothing<br />
except feeding her family.</p>
<p>Sorry to bring up Darfur,<br />
it just came to me—<br />
she’s a perfect example<br />
of living in the moment.</p>
<p>Hunger clamped to her breast,<br />
she can’t see<br />
beyond the present.<br />
She doesn’t know<br />
she can be anything, if only</p>
<p>she imagines hard enough.<br />
A surgeon for example,<br />
or an opera diva,<br />
or even president.</p>
<p>Some people are bad at imagining,<br />
always picturing the wrong things:<br />
sweeper of dirt floors, carrier of water jars,<br />
or maybe gatherer<br />
of twigs to cook mush.</p>
<p>Right now, I’m imagining<br />
I’m a giantess wearing a cornucopia<br />
headdress and a bra of gold coins.<br />
My laundry dries in the sun<br />
while I tan my washboard belly.</p>
<p>The more people have,<br />
the better they are<br />
at imagining. And that is why<br />
the rich get richer.</p>
</div>

**No Ethnic Identity**

<div class="poem-verse">
<p>It must be the Portuguese, it’s the Portuguese<br />
in you, kids would say about my olive skin<br />
not knowing anything about other countries<br />
or ethnicity.</p>
<p>We would say in school:<br />
the English had queens, the Scotch invented whiskey,<br />
the Irish had a famine, the French drank wine,<br />
the Germans made beer.<br />
The Portuguese had Magellan.</p>
<p>And once they all ruled<br />
all the continents and all the high seas,<br />
and they were great invaders and colonizers.<br />
That’s how we got here.</p>
<p>Only the Indians had reservations<br />
and my Portuguese was Azores.<br />
My great-grandpa was a whaler and great-grandma was a tailor.<br />
In eighteen-something, they arrived from Pico and Faial (no one knew how<br />
to spell these), came through Ellis Island,</p>
<p>settled in Rhode Island.<br />
My father’s family came mid-seventeen hundreds<br />
from someplace in Germany, landed somewhere<br />
in Pennsylvania. His father left Texas at twelve</p>
<p>and hopped a train. He was part English, French,<br />
Scotch-Irish. Somebody came from Ulster, somebody<br />
married a girl, last name French.<br />
My mother’s-mother’s mother came from Galway,<br />
married a German tailor. Another tailor in the family.</p>
<p>They somehow arrived at Eureka!<br />
Had ten kids. Few records, names and places sketchy.<br />
My maternal grandpa drove a truck for People’s Bakery<br />
in Berkeley. He was a card-carrying Commie.</p>
<p>On the side, he raised chickens and shoveled manure.<br />
Played ragtime piano on Saturday nights.<br />
A sister farmed apricots in San Jose.<br />
Some went to Canada, some came back.</p>
<p>Some went to Kansas then on to Oklahoma<br />
where they ran for land. Some eked along<br />
the high plains to Colorado. Some wound up in Oakland,<br />
worked for Southern Pacific and for Ma Bell.</p>
<p>Sewing, sowing, earth grubbing, homesteading<br />
in thin soil at high altitudes, the Government<br />
boasted was fertile and rich; in deep loam at low<br />
altitudes, the Government said was salty<br />
and best left fallow. Dirt roads turned to blacktop</p>
<p>everywhere. Great opportunity. If only the seeds<br />
would stay put and take root.<br />
This is only a fraction of the story,<br />
never mind service in the military.</p>
<p>I forgot this: My father said<br />
we had a little Indian. Maybe<br />
Cherokee or Sac and Fox, he thought.<br />
Not enough for any money or land.<br />
Later I learned: the Indian part came from Sarah Fox,</p>
<p>who was the common-law wife of my great<br />
great-great-great grandfather on my father’s mother’s side,<br />
and she bore fourteen. A twenty-foot obelisk marks<br />
her husband’s grave near Stillwater. Sarah’s name<br />
etched in miniscule lettering on the back hints at</p>
<p>woes endured and stories scattered. This hasn’t mattered.<br />
I have no ethnic identity.</p>
</div>

**Deflation**

<div class="poem-verse">
<p>Look, have toys dropped? (Pan to Zodiac Barbie,<br />
a real killer doll.)<br />
Is dining out more within reach? (Zoom in<br />
on triple bypass burger.)</p>
<p>Are car loans more attainable? (Pan to SUV<br />
with cowcatcher grill.)<br />
Has everything become cheaper?<br />
That is, “more affordable?”</p>
<p>Bread is rising, yes,<br />
and dairies can’t skim profits off milk<br />
and meat cuts into a bigger chunk<br />
of your budget. Ok, and gas is volatile.</p>
<p>Is housing at least collapsing?<br />
Let’s hope rents are flat.</p>
</div>

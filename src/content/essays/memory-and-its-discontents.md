Every archive is also a series of decisions about what not to keep. The documents that survive into permanence do so because someone, at some moment, chose to preserve them — over other documents, at the expense of other records, in competition with other claims on storage and attention. Memory, whether individual or collective, is always selective. What is less often acknowledged is that it is also political.

## The Archive as Instrument

The state has always understood this. Regimes that seek to consolidate power are typically attentive to the archive — not because they are unusually interested in the past, but because they understand that control over what is remembered is a form of control over what can be imagined as possible. The destruction of records is not merely an administrative act. It is an epistemological one: a restructuring of what can be known, and therefore of what can be done.

The problem is not only destruction, however. It is also curation. The archive that selects, that arranges, that presents — that creates, through its very organization, a narrative about what matters — is exercising a form of power that is harder to name than outright erasure. The well-preserved archive of the wrong things can be as distorting as no archive at all.

## What the Body Remembers

There is a form of memory that does not pass through the archive. It lives in the body — in gesture, in reflex, in the muscle's knowledge of how to perform a task it learned long ago. It lives in language — in the idioms that carry the residue of forgotten contexts, in the metaphors that crystallize a world-view no one explicitly holds anymore. It lives in the built environment — in streets that bear the names of figures whose significance has been lost, in buildings designed for functions that no longer exist.

This embodied, diffuse, largely unconscious memory is both more durable than the archive and more vulnerable to disruption. It cannot be burned. But it can be slowly erased — through the redesign of space, the replacement of idiom, the gradual dissolution of the communities that carry it.

## On the Uses of Forgetting

Not all forgetting is a loss. There are things worth forgetting — wounds that calcify into grievances, identities that become prisons, stories that sustain the conditions for their own repetition. The insistence on perfect memory, on the perpetual re-enactment of injury, can become its own form of politics — one that serves, sometimes, the interests of those who benefit from keeping old conflicts alive.

But this acknowledgment carries its own danger. The call to forget, to move on, to let the past be past, is among the most reliable instruments of those who benefit from the erasure of what they have done. To name this is not to oppose all forgetting. It is to insist that the question of what to remember and what to release cannot be settled in advance — that it is, like all questions of significance, a matter for ongoing, contestable, political judgment.

The past is not simply there, waiting to be recovered. It is made, and remade, in the present. What we make of it is a question about who we are willing to be.

**i. The Bread**

She called it by a word that doesn't translate. Not "bread" exactly — bread is too general, too abstract, too much about category. The word she used was specific to a particular loaf, a particular weight, the particular smell of a particular kitchen in a particular decade. When she moved, she kept using the word, though nothing it named existed anymore. This is what words do: they survive what they were for.

---

**ii. The Name**

His name had been changed twice — once when he was too young to protest, once when he was old enough to choose. He kept the second name until the end of his life, even after it stopped fitting in the way names that belong to you are supposed to fit. He wore it like someone else's coat.

When he thought of himself, he used neither name. He used a sound that wasn't quite a word — something pre-verbal, something from before the naming, something that had survived both changes.

We all have these, he said once. The sound you are before you are named.

---

**iii. The Greeting**

Every morning in the market square, the same exchange. *How are you. Fine. And you. Fine. Good morning. Good morning.* The words worn smooth from use, emptied of inquiry. But not meaningless. The meaning is precisely in the emptiness — in the performance of ordinary presence, the daily confirmation that both parties are here, are continuing, are participating in the minimal social contract of acknowledgment.

I am here. I see that you are here. This is a form of care.

---

**iv. The Insult**

Certain words carry histories their users have forgotten. The insult that began as a description of an occupation. The neutral term that calcified into contempt over centuries of use. Language remembers what the speakers have stopped knowing.

To speak is to be inhabited by history. To speak carefully is to try to know what you are inhabited by.

---

**v. Bread Again**

She still uses the word.

Sometimes the person she's with knows what she means. Sometimes they don't, and she has to explain, and the explanation is always insufficient. But she uses the word anyway.

This is also what words do: they insist on the presence of what is absent.

This is what words are for.

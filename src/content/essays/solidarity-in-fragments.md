Solidarity is not agreement. This seems obvious when stated plainly, yet it is constantly forgotten in practice — particularly by those who find themselves in positions of relative comfort, extending an offer of alliance that comes with the implicit condition that the offered-to will align themselves with the offerer's agenda, vocabulary, and sense of priority.

Real solidarity is harder than this. It involves showing up for people whose situation you do not fully understand, whose choices you might not make, whose politics may not map onto yours. It involves accepting, at least provisionally, someone else's account of their own experience — which may contradict your own — as the relevant ground for action. It is uncomfortable. It is often slow. It does not produce the clean feeling of having done the right thing.

## The Fragment as Form

I want to suggest that the fragmentary character of solidarity — its refusal to resolve into a smooth unity of purpose and identity — is not a defect but a feature. The dream of seamless solidarity, of a movement in which everyone agrees on everything and acts with one voice, is not only unrealistic. It is, in a specific way, dangerous.

Movements that achieve a high degree of internal uniformity tend to do so through exclusion — by silencing dissent, by making the costs of disagreement high enough that those who disagree leave or fall silent. The apparent coherence of such movements is purchased at the price of the very people they purport to represent: those whose situations don't fit the dominant narrative, whose needs complicate the clean story, whose presence introduces the friction that the movement would prefer to avoid.

Fragments are not failure. They are evidence that real people, with real differences, are present.

## What Solidarity Requires

It requires listening — which is harder than it sounds, because listening that is actually receptive, that allows what it hears to change what it thinks, is a form of vulnerability that most of us resist. It requires the willingness to be wrong, to have your assessment of a situation corrected by someone closer to it. It requires patience with the pace of others' movements, rather than the insistence that the urgency you feel should organize everyone's time.

None of this is comfortable. But discomfort is not, in itself, a sign that something has gone wrong. It may be a sign that something is actually happening — that contact has been made between differently situated people, and that the contact is doing the work contact is supposed to do.

Solidarity in fragments is still solidarity. It is perhaps the only kind that can last.

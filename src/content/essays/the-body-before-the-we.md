One of the most persistent assumptions in modern political thought is that politics begins with speech. The political subject is conventionally imagined as an individual who possesses an already constituted identity, interests, and demands and who subsequently enters the public sphere in order to articulate them. From this perspective, political action follows a relatively straightforward sequence: a subject becomes conscious of a condition, formulates a claim, communicates it, and joins others who recognize themselves in the same claim. Yet a significant body of contemporary political and social theory has complicated this model by asking a more elementary question: what makes collective political action possible before a political demand can be articulated in language?

Judith Butler, André Lepecki, Erin Manning, Brian Massumi, and Henri Lefebvre, despite the considerable differences among their theoretical projects, provide important resources for thinking about this problem. Their work directs attention toward bodily co-presence, movement, affect, spatial occupation, temporality, and rhythmic coordination as conditions through which political subjectivity can emerge. From this perspective, the political may begin before subjects speak: in the moment when bodies appear together, occupy a space, establish relations with one another, and acquire capacities that were not available to them in isolation.

This proposition does not require us to establish a simple opposition between embodiment and language. Political speech remains indispensable to collective life: movements need ways of naming experiences, formulating demands, remembering conflicts, communicating across distance, and reflecting upon their own practices. At the same time, speech itself depends upon material conditions that are easily rendered invisible when political agency is imagined primarily as an act of articulation. The speaking body must be able to appear, to remain, to move, to be heard, and to act alongside others. A political claim therefore emerges within a material field of bodies, spaces, infrastructures, temporal arrangements, and relations of dependence.

Judith Butler's account of assembly provides perhaps the most direct formulation of this problem. In *Notes Toward a Performative Theory of Assembly* and in her writings on bodies in public space, Butler challenges the assumption that collective action simply expresses an already existing political subject. When bodies assemble, their presence is itself politically consequential. They do not merely enter a public space in order to communicate a message; through gathering, remaining, and acting together, they can alter the meaning and function of that space. The assembly participates in producing the public character of the space in which it appears.

This has significant consequences for the theory of political subjectivity. The collective does not necessarily precede collective action. Under certain conditions, it is generated through action itself. Individuals who arrive at a demonstration, occupy a square, gather in a street, or stand together against an imposed order may not possess a fully articulated collective identity beforehand. Their political capacity develops through relations of mutual dependence. Bodies support one another physically, sustain one another's presence, and create the conditions under which collective persistence becomes possible.

Butler thereby complicates the classical liberal image of the autonomous political subject. The subject is commonly represented as an independent individual who subsequently enters into political relations. Human bodies, however, are constitutively dependent upon other bodies, infrastructures, institutions, and material environments. Political agency consequently cannot be reduced to individual intention. To appear together is already to expose the body to others and to make a claim concerning who has the right to appear, remain, move, and occupy space.

This becomes particularly important in situations where certain populations are systematically rendered invisible or politically inaudible. When people whose voices have been excluded from official political discourse assemble publicly, their physical presence can function as a political assertion before any proposition has been formulated. *We are here* is not simply a statement that assembled bodies may subsequently pronounce; it is something they enact through their presence. The political meaning of the utterance derives partly from the fact that the bodies have already made themselves present within a space in which their presence may have been contested.

André Lepecki develops a related argument through the concept of choreopolitics. Drawing on Jacques Rancière's distinction between politics and the police, Lepecki asks how bodies are distributed in space and how movement itself participates in the reproduction or disruption of established regimes of social organization. The question is therefore not merely what a dance represents, but what forms of movement it permits, prohibits, organizes, renders visible, or makes impossible.

This shift is significant because choreography is never simply a matter of aesthetic form. To choreograph bodies is to arrange relations between them: who moves, who remains still, who follows, who leads, who occupies the centre, who is marginalised, who becomes visible, and who is required to remain outside the field of perception. Choreography can consequently be understood as a condensed model of social organization. Its political dimension becomes particularly apparent when movement itself is regulated as part of the organization of collective life.

Lepecki's concept of choreopolitics emerges at the point where movement ceases to reproduce an established distribution of bodies and begins to generate other possibilities. Political movement, in this sense, does not depend upon representing political ideas through dance. It can involve the production of different relations among bodies through movement itself. A collective may therefore experiment bodily with forms of social relation before those relations have been translated into an institutional programme or an explicit political discourse.

This perspective also requires caution. Collective movement has no inherent political direction, and the gathering of bodies does not automatically constitute resistance. Choreography can reproduce discipline as effectively as it can interrupt it. Military formations, industrial routines, institutional spaces, and nationalist spectacles all organize bodies through movement and rhythm. The political question therefore concerns the organization of movement itself: the relations through which bodies are coordinated, the capacities such coordination creates, and the forms of hierarchy or autonomy that emerge from it.

Erin Manning pushes the argument further by challenging the assumption that the individual subject necessarily precedes relation. Her work on movement, perception, affect, and collective becoming suggests that subjectivity can emerge within relations rather than existing fully formed before them. The individual is not always the starting point from which relations subsequently develop; in many situations, relations participate in producing the individual as a particular kind of subject.

This becomes especially visible in movement. When bodies move together, their movements modify one another. A gesture anticipates another gesture; a change of rhythm alters the movement of the group; proximity creates possibilities that distance would not have permitted; the collective configuration generates capacities that no participant possessed in precisely the same form while acting alone. What appears as individual movement is therefore frequently constituted through a field of relational adjustment.

The political implications are considerable. If subjectivity is relationally produced, political agency cannot be adequately understood as the expression of pre-existing individual identities. Collective agency may emerge through coordination, responsiveness, improvisation, and mutual transformation. Politics concerns what subjects consciously demand, certainly, but it also concerns what bodies become capable of doing through their relations with one another.

Brian Massumi's work on affect provides another dimension to this problem. His account of affect, drawing extensively on Spinoza and Deleuze, emphasizes bodily capacities that cannot be exhausted by conscious intention or explicit representation. Before an experience becomes a fully articulated thought, it may already have modified the body's capacity to act, to respond, or to enter into relation with other bodies.

This does not place affect outside language or politics. Rather, it draws attention to processes through which political possibilities take shape before they become fully conscious or verbally articulated. Bodies are continually affected by other bodies, spaces, rhythms, sounds, gestures, and movements. These encounters can increase or diminish their capacity for action, alter their orientation toward others, and change their sense of what is possible within a given situation.

From this perspective, political subjectivity cannot be explained entirely through rational agreement. A collective may acquire a sense of possibility through the affective intensities generated by being together. Confidence, fear, exhilaration, vulnerability, hesitation, anger, and solidarity are not merely psychological states accompanying political action from the outside. They participate in determining what bodies are capable of doing and what kinds of relations they can sustain.

This also helps explain why collective political action cannot be understood solely through explicit ideological agreement. People do not always become politically active because they have first reached an identical theoretical understanding of their situation. Sometimes a common capacity becomes perceptible only through the experience of acting together. The political collective can therefore be an affective and embodied achievement whose conceptual vocabulary develops retrospectively, through the attempt to understand what has already begun to take place.

Henri Lefebvre's theory of rhythm provides another way of conceptualizing this collective dimension. In *Rhythmanalysis*, social life is understood through the interaction of bodily, spatial, temporal, and social rhythms. Human beings inhabit rhythms: repetitions, interruptions, accelerations, pauses, cycles, and forms of synchronization through which everyday existence acquires its temporal organization.

This is particularly relevant to the political significance of collective movement. To act together often requires the establishment of a shared rhythm. Bodies walking together, marching together, singing together, dancing together, or occupying a space together must continually negotiate temporal relations. They respond to one another and develop a common pace without necessarily becoming identical.

Rhythm therefore offers a material conception of solidarity. Solidarity need not initially take the form of agreement upon a proposition. It can also involve the ability of bodies to sustain a common temporal relation. A collective exists partly through the forms of mutual responsiveness that allow its members to remain coordinated over time, even when their individual positions, capacities, and experiences differ.

The distinction is important. A crowd is not automatically a political subject. Bodies gathered together may remain atomized, passive, or subordinated to an external organization. What matters is the emergence of a capacity for coordination. The collective becomes politically significant when bodies begin to generate relations of movement, attention, support, and persistence that cannot be understood simply as the sum of individual intentions.

Taken together, these approaches suggest a substantial revision of the traditional sequence through which political agency is imagined. Rather than assuming a linear movement from individual subjectivity to consciousness, speech, and collective action, we might attend to a more complex process involving bodily presence, relation, shared space, movement, affective and rhythmic coordination, collective capacity, and political articulation.

This should not be treated as a new chronological formula. Political processes rarely unfold in such an orderly manner. Speech can produce new forms of bodily assembly just as bodily assembly can produce new forms of speech. A political concept can reorganize the ways bodies move through a space, while a transformation in bodily relations can make previously unavailable forms of political articulation possible. The point is therefore less to establish a temporal sequence than to insist that political language rests upon material conditions that it cannot fully contain or explain.

The political body consequently exceeds the body that speaks. It is also the body that stands, walks, dances, occupies, waits, touches, supports, withdraws, approaches, and remains beside another body. These actions acquire political significance when the distribution of bodies itself becomes contested. The question of who may appear, where, under what conditions, and for how long is already a question concerning the organization of social life.

Such an account also transforms our understanding of solidarity. Solidarity is often represented as an agreement among already constituted individuals who recognize that they share a political interest. Embodied theories of collective action suggest a different possibility: solidarity can emerge through the practical organization of relations among bodies. People may discover that they are capable of acting together before possessing a stable language for describing what that togetherness means. The collective relation can therefore precede its explicit conceptualization.

This does not make embodied solidarity politically sufficient. Political transformation requires institutions, organization, discourse, memory, and sustained collective practices. Yet these dimensions of political life do not emerge independently of embodied relations. They have to be materially reproduced through the capacities of people to remain present to one another, to coordinate action, to endure disagreement, and to sustain forms of common life beyond the intensity of an initial political encounter.

The same point can be extended to the question of political organization. Institutions are often treated as structures that stand above embodied action, but their durability depends upon the repeated participation of bodies within them. Meetings require presence; organization requires communication; collective memory requires practices through which experiences are preserved and transmitted; political continuity depends upon the ability of people to reproduce relations over time. Even highly abstract forms of political organization remain dependent upon material capacities that cannot be reduced to the formal structures themselves.

The significance of the body in contemporary political theory therefore lies in the possibility of relocating the beginning of politics. Politics does not necessarily begin with an already constituted subject formulating a demand. It can begin through the emergence of a capacity to appear together, to occupy a space, to establish relations, to develop a rhythm, to affect one another, and to sustain a common movement.

This does not diminish the importance of political speech. It places speech within a wider field of material relations. A political claim acquires force through the conditions that allow it to be uttered, heard, repeated, remembered, and collectively sustained. The capacity to speak politically is itself partly produced through forms of embodied association.

From this perspective, solidarity may be understood less as a final state of consensus than as an ongoing practice of sustaining relations among different bodies. It involves responsiveness, adjustment, mutual dependence, and the capacity to remain together without requiring the complete disappearance of difference. The collective does not become politically meaningful because its members cease to be distinct; its political potential may lie precisely in the forms of coordination through which difference can be sustained without dissolving the possibility of common action.

There is also an important temporal dimension to this argument. Collective political capacity cannot be reduced to the intensity of the moment in which bodies first assemble. Movements endure through repetition: assemblies are held again, spaces are revisited, memories are transmitted, rhythms are established, conflicts are negotiated, and relations are reconstructed after moments of rupture. In this sense, political subjectivity is not simply something that emerges once. It has to be continually reproduced.

The body, then, is one of the places from which politics emerges, but it is also one of the places where political possibilities are sustained, transformed, and sometimes lost. To attend to embodiment is to attend to the material conditions through which a collective becomes capable of acting and to the temporal processes through which that capacity acquires durability.

Before a collective can articulate a common demand, it may have to develop the material capacity to remain together. Before it can recognize itself as a political subject, relations of interdependence may already have begun to produce that subject. And before political subjectivity becomes an articulated identity, it may already exist as a fragile, provisional, corporeal capacity to act in common.

The political, in this sense, may begin earlier than its own language allows us to see.

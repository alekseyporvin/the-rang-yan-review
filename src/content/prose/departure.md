He had rented a van, which turned out to be larger than necessary. His father had not been a collector of things. The apartment — two rooms on the third floor of a building near the river — contained a bed, a table, four chairs, a bookshelf that was mostly empty, a wardrobe, and a kitchen with equipment for a man who ate simply. There was also, in a box under the bed, a collection of photographs and a few documents. This was everything.

Pawel stood in the middle of the main room for a while before he started to carry things down. He was aware that he was behaving with a kind of deliberateness — moving slowly, touching objects lightly before picking them up — that he would not have attributed to himself. He did not think of himself as a man who attached significance to objects. But something in the room required this slowness.

The chair at the table, for instance. His father had sat in this chair — in the evenings, after dinner, sometimes reading, sometimes just sitting with his hands on the table in the particular way he had: palms flat, fingers slightly spread, as if he were feeling the surface for something underneath it. Pawel had seen him sit this way hundreds of times and had never once asked what he was thinking.

---

The neighbor from across the corridor came out as Pawel was loading the second trip. She was an older woman who had lived there for decades and had known his father slightly — well enough to exchange greetings, to accept the occasional piece of mail, to knock on his door once when she heard nothing for three days.

"He was a quiet man," she said. This was offered as a kind of condolence, though Pawel was not certain it was one.

"Yes," he said.

"He used to sit on the staircase sometimes. In the evenings. Just sitting."

Pawel looked at the staircase. He had never been here in the evenings.

"Did he say anything?" he asked.

"Sometimes. About the weather. Once about a book he was reading." She paused. "I didn't ask. I should have asked."

---

He took the chair.

He didn't need a chair. He had furniture. But the chair was the thing he could not leave in the apartment to be disposed of by the building management, donated to a secondhand shop, sat in by someone who didn't know what it was.

It would not fit well in his apartment. It would occupy a corner without a purpose. He would move it occasionally, looking for a place where it made sense, and it would never quite make sense, and eventually he would accept this.

On the motorway back to the city, the van was much too large for what it contained.

The fields on either side were flat and brown. The sky was the color of old paper.

Pawel drove without the radio on, which was not his habit. He drove for three hours like this, thinking of nothing he could name — just the road, and the fields, and the sense of carrying something that was not heavy but could not be put down.

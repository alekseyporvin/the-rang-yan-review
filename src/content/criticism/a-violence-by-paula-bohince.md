<p class="review-imprint"><em>A Violence</em> by Paula Bohince. 88 pp. Princeton University Press, 2025. $17.95.</p>

<div class="essay-verse essay-verse--inset">
<p>… Healing,</p>
<p>the literature says, is relational. As the wound was.</p>
<p class="essay-verse__cite">(“Pathway”)</p>
</div>

*A Violence* is a collection agonised by the struggle to relate. This manifests both in the poet’s yearning for communion with other people and the wider world, and in the poetry’s attempt to adequately confess the origins and consequences of trauma. The poems are rangy, reaching across borders of material and voice. We find ourselves by turns in a muddy old-country homestead, swimming in the ocean off the coast of Fiji, drinking in smoky bars and cocktail lounges, and eating ice cream in Cornwall. The reader seeking secure footing in any of these locations will flounder. They are not to be understood as mainland or home soil: rather, they are islands engulfed by the “surrealist’s melt” of traumatised dissociation (“Pillow Talk”).

There is, however, nothing wishy-washy about these poems. Bohince’s achievement is to maintain precision of language and image even in verse which enacts the disorienting mirage of PTSD. The collection coheres through exactly the kind of intrusive recurrences that characterise traumatised existence. Pervasive among these is the “Double cream of / childhood amnesia” (“Sumptuary”). Poems such as “The Lambs are Not for Sale” show their workings, inviting the reader to consider creamy amnesia as a binding agent that yokes the qualities of people and objects to one another. Lambs “panting after / mothers, arched under the creamy ceilings of / clouds-in-frescoes bellies, / … curl into the porcelain figures / your grandmother kept on her dresser”. They stir memories of “milk-carton children” with “crooked haircuts / and freckles” that both “were and were not you” and which, in time, “froze as milk did / on winter’s doorstep”.

Subsequent iterations – a spider lounging “on the cream of my blouse” (“I Love the Whole World”), an “apple tart with cream made for your hospital homecoming” (“Pillow Talk”), the “milky flowers” expressed from a mother’s nipples (“Black Swans”) – are elevated by this binding schema: they take on a semiotic quality, pointing beyond surface nostalgia to a “mothering quiet” (“I Love the Whole World”) which, unlike milk, cannot be expressed. This woolly childhood idyll is, of course, not to be trusted. Marie-Antoinette, the reader is informed, “made a similar wonderland / in Petit Trianon, nursery of girlhood innocence, / and her children were still killed”. This collision of nostalgia with brute reality leaves only one viable course:

<div class="essay-verse essay-verse--inset">
<p>Hold fast to the fantasy: their inviolate curls</p>
<p>the foam of seashore summers, wished-on fluff of dying</p>
<p>dandelions, the eyelet of a christening gown</p>
<p>pulled over your infant eyes.</p>
<p class="essay-verse__cite">(“The Lambs are Not for Sale”)</p>
</div>

If the fantasy is to be sustained, the wool must remain over the eyes. The “foam of seashore summers” provides a further means of obfuscation. In the collection’s opening poem “Accordian Music” we encounter “my doomed / daddy in clover, in blown beer foam / like dandelions in July”. In “Among Barmaids” the poet swims through the booze of pitiable men, serving “Froth pillowed up / from underground barrels”, while in “Glass of Milk” a grieving mother vanishes “into a strobing, off-kilter rainstorm, / the frothy whitecaps of a harbour’s embrace and resistance”.

Foam also performs an explicitly therapeutic function. In “Epic Rain” a soothing sleep track manifests delta waves resembling “the ones at sea, / after catastrophe, / a civilisation’s treasures floating / unencumbered”. This jostle of artifacts “tumbling through foam” is “the brain / on benzos and earphones, frictionless / and plugged in, miraculously, / to the seafloor’s fiber optics.”

If these fibre-optic dreams are initially beguiling, in “Albino Deer” they are atomised into the attention economy’s weaponisation of Tennyson’s “topmost froth of thought” (*In Memoriam*, Section LII): “White noise, attention span frail as a ghost crab / clattering into surf, washed backward into the mist / of Ansel’s photographs, synth to soften a century, / gallows clouds fusing with Osipova’s jetés in the Bolshoi.” This is the surf through which the traumatised mind is “Distracted from distraction by distraction” (Eliot, “Burnt Norton”) and non-digital connection is kept at arm’s length.

In “Search Field” the poet admits to being “safest in quiet one-way / meetings, sitting like a spider at the centre of a web, watching it tremble”. From this vantage point, the “flock of sheep in the train window” seems unreal: “Five field-seconds perfected by fog, duffle snuggled against me / like a child, the wash of creamy daubs against green, / then gone.” While travelling along this “rail of fibre optics” might “quell the panic”, it does nothing to slow the “accumulation of wool in the past”.

Swimming in this “blurry *before*” (“A Violence”) it is “the doe / (*always, always*)” of “Lamentation Once Again” that the poet comes back to: “Her guileless, / maternal face, her breast a candle against the christening dress / of vapor”. This mother is not equipped to protect the “fawn born / too soon, too weak” from “the *why* dooming us gently, hope and pain in every utterance of that / hurt, over and over unanswered”. In response

<div class="essay-verse essay-verse--inset">
<p>…the traumatized</p>
<p>child’s amygdala reacts, gripped by</p>
<p>permanent panic,</p>
<p class="essay-verse__break" aria-hidden="true"></p>
<p>grown weird in the woods</p>
<p>among broke iris, introvert</p>
<p>birches, the cones</p>
<p>of her eyes sorting <em>frond</em></p>
<p>from <em>danger</em>, repainting the world,</p>
<p>the first memory.</p>
<p class="essay-verse__cite">(“A Violence”)</p>
</div>

It is this first memory of abandonment amongst the violence of “too much spring” that the surfeit of cream and foam attempts to soothe. The result is stunted growth, the inability to move on from a childhood that never happened. “I’ve held on to / childishness longer than most” the poet confesses in the closing lines of “The Green”, the collection’s final poem: “green loss, / the green, Oh God, it is the cause”. There is a self-flagellating unkindness in these lines. They convey a crushing sense of responsibility for one’s own pain that another poet might have placed outside of themselves, in the external factors of hurt. Bohince is not that kind of poet: she squarely confronts the habits of obfuscation that impede meaningful healing. This is poetry which takes responsibility for itself, which moves beyond the disclosure of instigating wounds and accounts for the ways in which wounded people become complicit in their own pain. In today’s economy of hurt this is a brave and commendable position to take, the result of a profound reckoning that has no truck with the platitudes of life-hack healing.

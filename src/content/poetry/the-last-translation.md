Every translation is an act of faith
in a language you do not believe in.

You carry the original
like a bowl of water —
across the room, across the century,
across the difference between
what the word meant then
and what the word can mean now.

Some of it spills.
You know this going in.
You proceed anyway.

---

There is a word in the language I was given —
a word for the grief that is also gratitude,
for the goodbye that contains the memory
of the hello —

and when I reach for its equivalent
in the language I chose,
I find nothing. A gap. An apology.
The footnote that says: *untranslatable.*

---

But I have learned to distrust footnotes.
Every word, translated honestly,
carries its own untranslatability —
its history, its accidents,
the mouths that shaped it
into what it is.

The untranslatable is not the exception.
It is the condition.

---

I translate anyway.

Not to capture but to reach —
to make a bridge that crosses the river
while acknowledging the river is too wide,
the bridge is approximate,
the other shore is not quite where I said it was.

The crossing is the work.
The reaching is the meaning.

---

The last translator of a dying language
works without a reader in mind.
She translates to keep the language alive
one more day,
one more paragraph,
one more way of saying
what the other language cannot say.

I think of her when I sit
with the original in my left hand
and the page blank before me.

I think: faithfulness is not equivalence.
Faithfulness is trying.

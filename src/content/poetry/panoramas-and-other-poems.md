<p><strong>Panoramas</strong></p>

<pre class="poem-lines">The day slowly passes like one of those
ramshackle and crowded buses.
The children unleash their homemade kites
for Aeolus to frolic with them.
But the God is tired, so he tangles
them on the powerlines and antennas.

The women put on the dresses
they bought from some Haitians,
and the flower sellers give them
the last bouquets of roses for free
that they sell at stoplights.

The dog trying to extract the flattened
rat from the asphalt with his claws,
makes me think about my efforts to
recollect the past and my childhood.

Meanwhile, the winds flutter through
the drapes as if they were wings of cranes,
and on the rooftops people collect shirts,
pants, and sheets from the clotheslines.

The sun sets, leaving myself, the city,
and these words lurching.
Trucks roll through the avenue
with their lights off,
and we gesture at them to turn on their lights,
and when the truck drivers do so
the objects we once thought in shambles and rickety
acquire a certain beauty.

Shopping carts creak
in the supermarket aisles, and in the streets
the sirens from ambulances mix
with those of patrol cars.

Up above, the moon resembles the head
of a shark that fishermen
once cut and placed at the entrance
of the neighborhood to teach the unwary a lesson.</pre>

<p class="poem-next"><strong>The Barista</strong></p>

<pre class="poem-lines">In Washington D.C. there is a café my brother frequents
every morning before heading to the office.
Sometimes the line gets long because the barista
is deaf-mute and it takes her some time to read the lips

of clients, and these customers, progressive,
civilized, and middle-aged, speak to her slowly
and with tenderness so that she can read their words,
although of course, from time to time she makes

a mistake, which happened to my brother, as she
brewed him a café americano instead of a macchiato
with almond milk.  We don’t think
enough about the importance of baristas in

our lives and how they procure us the dosage
of caffeine we require to get through the grind.
Baristas are up there with one’s therapist or
astrologer, drug dealer or alchemist.

A barista in Boston told me how some goats
from Ethiopia often eat the fruits of the coffee plants,
and then defecate the grains and that with those grains
one brews the priciest coffee in the world.

Last week my brother sent me a photo
of the deaf-mute barista: they selected her as
employee of the month, and the line for coffee
continues expanding, as everyone’s thrilled, hoping

she’ll prepare their daily dosage of caffeine,
and I will take a flight to see my brother, and we’ll
wait in line so that she can read my lips the way
many silently read the words on this page.</pre>

<p class="poem-next"><strong>Playing Hoops in my Forties</strong></p>

<pre class="poem-lines">Playing hoops in my forties with kids
who, chronologically, could be my children
and who shout out Mister, pass me the ball,

and though I am cautious, and I have altered my
pace at playing from fear of impacts, tumbles,
scraped knees, busted hips, or rolled ankles,

it is also true that I have maintained my stride
at swishing the hoop, making rebounds like that bearded
man with whom we played when I was a teen.

During one game he fell over the ball like
Saul from the saddle en route to Damascus,
and just like Saul he was changed forever:

fractured hip, ruptured disc, three broken ribs, not
to mention the emergency-room bill, all clinched
his fear at placing a toe on the court

as if it were a peak’s topmost crag.  And I share
this anecdote because I have transformed
into that bearded man; I still leap and clap


when my teammates score, and whenever
possible I stretch, moreover, the kids see me as
some father-figure—God willing, not their Gramps—

and I do squats, push-ups, sit-ups because I know
that the fall is fast-approaching, and while
the kids cross the hoop as rapid as

the Achaean phalanxes, I limp to the shower stalls,
roll down my kneepads, bandages, and
wristbands, as if they were my armor,

and I were King Agamemnon gazing at himself
once more in the mirror, asking if what’s
glazing my face are tears, blood, or sweat.</pre>

<p class="poem-next"><strong>Backpack</strong></p>

<pre class="poem-lines">That night I left my closet door
open and listened to my backpack’s snoring.
It was tossed inside some years ago, forgotten.
It was once my shell, my house.

I crossed Paris lugging it on my back
like Jean Valjean and Marius in <em>Les Miserables</em>,
whispering to him to not lose hope, two blocks
and we’ll reach the arms of Cosette, your sweetheart.

Now it was there in the darkness, lonely,
snoring and dreaming and perhaps dreaming
that I looked for it in the closet
like a snail looking for its shell,

or maybe tossing and turning from a nightmare
in which I rejected it and was embarrassed by it,
and now regarded it as a mute hump
that grew on my back.

I heard it snoring.  All the sleepless
night I heard it snoring.
I didn’t dare get up to close
the closet door.  I feared waking it up.</pre>

<p class="poem-next"><strong>After the Beach</strong></p>

<pre class="poem-lines">After returning from the beach, we lie down and our horizontal
bodies imagine that we are still floating.

We have no idea whether we are in bed or on water.
Obliging, we let the tide of dreams drag us

like offerings to Yemayá*, like rafts, like algae.
From my shore, I perceive your struggle against the current

as if you were a jellyfish trapped in the nets,
but instead of nets, perhaps it’s a matter of the sheets

tangled between your thighs and your back.
Upon rolling over, it’s as if a wave were to sweep you away,

and with my legs and arms I clutch you to stop it
from taking you while the ocean’s jaws devour you.

Just like two castaways, we hug one another, and it doesn’t matter
where we find ourselves, we will remain steadfast and urge

on each other’s strokes to keep ourselves afloat, the same
as two buoys on the surface of this bed as vast as the sea.</pre>

<p><em>*A water deity in the Yoruba religion.</em></p>

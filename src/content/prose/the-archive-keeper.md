The records room was on the fourth floor of the old administrative building, at the end of a corridor that smelled of dust and something older than dust — the particular smell of paper that had absorbed decades of human anxiety and then been left alone to process it in silence.

Irina worked there from seven-thirty in the morning until four in the afternoon, five days a week, with Wednesdays off. She had been doing this for eleven years. She knew the filing system better than its creator had, better than the director of the institution, better than the young assistants who occasionally came upstairs to retrieve a record and left looking shaken by the quantity of accumulated paper.

She was not sentimental about it. The records were records: thin cardboard folders, typed and handwritten forms, photographs in some cases, official stamps in others. They represented people, but they were not people. They were evidence that people had existed and had passed through a system — which was not the same as a history of those people, not the same as a portrait, not the same as a memory. Irina understood this distinction clearly, which was one of the reasons she was good at her job.

---

The woman who came in on a Thursday afternoon in late November was not the kind of visitor Irina usually received. Most people who came upstairs were professionals — social workers, administrators, occasionally a legal representative. This woman wore a coat that had been expensive once, and she stood in the doorway for a long moment before entering, as if she needed to give the room permission to receive her.

"I'm looking for a record," the woman said. "For my mother."

Irina put down her pen. "Your mother's name?"

"That's the thing." The woman pressed her lips together. "I'm not entirely certain."

---

It took two hours to find what the woman was looking for — not the file she'd asked for, which didn't exist, but the file that turned out to be related to it, that pointed sideways to another name, another entry, another folder that the woman held in her hands with a specific kind of stillness.

Irina watched her read.

She had watched many people read the records. It was, she had come to understand, a particular kind of reading — slower than ordinary reading, more careful, shot through with something that was not quite hope but was in the same family. People who came upstairs looking for records were looking for something that could not really be found in the records: confirmation that someone had existed, had mattered, had been more than an absence in someone else's life.

The records could not confirm this. But they could confirm that someone had been registered, had been processed, had been here — and sometimes, for the person holding the folder, this was enough.

---

"Is this what you were looking for?" Irina asked.

The woman looked up. Her eyes were dry. "I don't know what I was looking for," she said. "But yes. This is it."

Irina filed the cross-reference she'd made during the search, noted the visit in the access log, and returned to the form she'd been working on before the woman arrived.

Outside, the city went about its business.

In the records room, eleven years of paper waited for the next question.
